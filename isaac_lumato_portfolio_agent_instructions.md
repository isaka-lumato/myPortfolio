# Agent Instructions: Build Isaac Lumato’s Creative Portfolio

## Your role

Act as a world-class creative director, digital designer, interaction designer, and senior frontend engineer. Design and build a polished, production-ready personal portfolio website for **Isaac Lumato**.

Do not make a generic developer portfolio, a template-like agency landing page, or a grid of disconnected projects. The website should feel like **a story carefully crafted into a portfolio**: cinematic, artistic, thoughtful, modern, human, and technically excellent.

Make strong design decisions and implement the site. Do not stop at a proposal, wireframe, or list of recommendations.

---

## 1. The central idea

### Brand
**Isaac Lumato**

### Professional identity
**Creative Technologist · AI Filmmaker · Software Builder**

### Core positioning
Isaac combines software development, artificial intelligence, filmmaking, motion, and storytelling to turn ambitious ideas into real experiences.

He is not simply someone who uses AI tools. He is a creative problem-solver who can imagine an idea, work out how to execute it with modern technology, and turn it into something people can see, use, or buy.

### Guiding thought
**Human imagination. Amplified by technology.**

### Suggested homepage statement
> Technology is powerful. Imagination is what makes it matter.

Supporting copy:
> I build with AI, create cinematic experiences, and turn complex technology into useful things.

Treat this as a creative direction, not text that must be used verbatim everywhere. Preserve the underlying meaning and voice.

### The emotional goal
Visitors should feel that they have entered the world of a curious, ambitious maker—someone who moves between technology and art without treating them as separate worlds. The website should reveal this person gradually, like a short film or a well-paced story.

The site should communicate:
- Creative judgment, not just tool familiarity.
- Technical ability backed by real work.
- Curiosity, experimentation, and a personal point of view.
- A connection to Tanzania and an ambition to make work that can travel globally.
- Approachability and a clear path to collaboration.

Do not present Isaac as an expert in everything. Confidence should come from evidence, specificity, and craft.

## 2. Creative direction and visual language

### Overall aesthetic
Create a dark, cinematic, editorial experience with the restraint of a premium independent film studio and the clarity of a modern software product.

The site should be artistic without becoming confusing, minimal without feeling empty, and animated without becoming exhausting.

### Color
Use a restrained palette:
- Near-black / charcoal as the primary canvas.
- Warm white or soft ivory for primary text.
- Muted grey for secondary text.
- Deep burgundy / maroon as a distinctive accent.
- Optional subtle warm highlights where they serve the work.

Avoid excessive gradients, neon cyberpunk colors, generic purple AI gradients, glassmorphism everywhere, and colorful cards for their own sake.

### Typography
Use a strong editorial pairing:
- A characterful display face for large headlines.
- A clean, highly legible sans-serif for body text, navigation, metadata, and controls.

Use large type intentionally. Create contrast through scale, spacing, line breaks, and rhythm—not through dozens of font sizes. Keep paragraphs readable and avoid excessively wide text columns.

### Composition
Use:
- Generous negative space.
- Strong alignment and carefully controlled asymmetry.
- Full-bleed visual moments when justified.
- Editorial project layouts with varied image proportions.
- Small, useful metadata labels.
- Thin rules and subtle separators.
- Layering and overlap only when they improve the story.
- Intentional transitions between quiet sections and visual impact.

Do not put every element inside a rounded card. Do not use a repetitive three-column grid as the answer to every layout problem.

### Imagery
Real work should take priority over decorative imagery.

Use actual project assets supplied in the repository or by the user whenever available. Do not fabricate screenshots, client work, awards, logos, products, or results.

If assets are missing, build elegant, intentional placeholders that are easy to replace. Clearly mark placeholder content in the code or content data. Do not imply generated concept imagery is actual client work.

Visual direction can include:
- Cinematic film stills.
- Realistic, atmospheric photography.
- Strong shadow and controlled light.
- Software interfaces shown in believable contexts.
- Contact sheets, frames, storyboards, timelines, and behind-the-scenes material where relevant.
- Abstract visuals only when they support a real idea.

Do not use random stock photos simply to fill empty space.

## 3. The website should unfold like a story

Treat the visitor’s journey as a narrative with a beginning, a reveal, evidence, experimentation, and an invitation.

### Chapter 1 — The opening frame
The first viewport should feel like the opening shot of a film.

Include:
- Isaac’s name as a clear wordmark.
- A minimal navigation.
- A strong, memorable headline.
- One concise sentence explaining the work.
- One primary call to action: **Explore the work**.
- One secondary call to action: **Let’s create something**.
- A striking visual moment that reflects the blend of filmmaking and technology.

Do not bury the message under an elaborate intro animation. The page must communicate its purpose immediately, even if motion fails or is disabled.

Consider a restrained moving film strip, a carefully art-directed video loop, or a layered still composition if real assets exist. If no suitable assets exist, create a beautiful typographic opening rather than a fake cinematic montage.

Navigation labels:
**Work · The Lab · About · Contact**

The header can be minimal and sticky if it genuinely helps navigation.

### Chapter 2 — The point of view
Before showing a large collection of work, communicate how Isaac thinks.

Possible message:
> I’m interested in what happens when human imagination meets new technology.

Use a short editorial passage, a striking typographic treatment, or a visual transition. Avoid a generic “I am passionate about innovation” paragraph.

This chapter should make the visitor curious about the work that follows.

### Chapter 3 — Selected work
This is the main evidence section. Make the projects feel like chapters in a curated collection, not items in a template.

Feature up to three strong projects initially:
1. A software system or product.
2. An AI-powered film or advertisement.
3. The strongest Inkryptus creative project.

Only include projects that can be supported by actual assets or accurate information. If a project is not ready to show, leave a clear content slot rather than inventing details.

Vary the layout based on each project’s visual character. A film project may deserve a large cinematic frame; a software product may deserve an interface preview and a clear explanation; a campaign may benefit from a storyboard or before/after presentation.

Each project entry should make it easy to understand:
- What it is.
- The challenge or opportunity.
- Isaac’s actual role and contribution.
- The creative or technical approach.
- The result, with verified evidence only.
- A way to explore the project further, where available.

Use short, compelling summaries on the homepage. Put deeper information on dedicated case-study pages or well-designed expandable project details.

Do not invent metrics, client endorsements, project outcomes, competition placements, or technology stacks. If information is unknown, omit it or mark it as content that needs to be supplied.

### Chapter 4 — A closer look
Create an editorial case-study experience for selected projects.

A case study should read like a short making-of story:
1. **The idea:** What was the challenge, and what did Isaac see that was interesting?
2. **The execution:** How did he approach it? Which creative choices and technologies mattered?
3. **The result:** Show the finished film, functioning product, live website, or verified outcome.
4. **The insight:** What was learned, changed, or discovered?

Use large media, concise writing, meaningful captions, and visual evidence. Avoid walls of text and generic process diagrams.

For Inkryptus, the potential narrative is how to communicate a complex financial or technology concept through a distinctive creative approach. Use the actual submitted work and only state competition achievements that can be verified.

### Chapter 5 — The Lab
This section represents Isaac’s curiosity and ongoing experimentation.

Possible categories:
- **Visual Experiments:** AI imagery, cinematic tests, and visual storytelling.
- **Motion & Animation:** Code-driven visuals, animation systems, and motion design.
- **AI Workflows:** Prototypes and experiments in building or creating with AI.
- **Stories Worth Telling:** Personal ideas about people, life, technology, and society.

The Lab should feel like a filmmaker’s notebook mixed with a creative research journal. It can be more informal than the selected work section, but it must still be art-directed.

Allow a small experiment to be published without requiring a full commercial case study. Use dates, short notes, a few visuals, and a concise explanation of what was explored.

Do not populate the Lab with fabricated experiments. If there are no supplied assets yet, show a graceful empty state or clearly identified sample layout that is easy to replace.

### Chapter 6 — The person behind the work
Make the About section human and concise.

Suggested copy, which may be refined for natural flow:

> I’m a creative technologist interested in what happens when human imagination meets new technology.
>
> My work moves between software development, AI-powered filmmaking, visual storytelling, and creative experimentation. I enjoy taking ideas that seem difficult, expensive, or unconventional and finding new ways to bring them to life.
>
> I don’t believe technology should replace creativity. I believe it should expand what creative people are capable of making.
>
> I’m particularly interested in building and creating from Tanzania, while working on ideas that can reach audiences far beyond it.
>
> **I build. I experiment. I tell stories. I’m still exploring what’s possible.**

Use a real portrait if available. Otherwise, a typographic treatment or a relevant genuine working image is better than a fabricated portrait.

Do not invent education, employers, job titles, years of experience, personal history, or biographical claims.

### Chapter 7 — The invitation
End with a strong, quiet, confident contact moment.

Suggested headline:
> Have an idea? Let’s build it.

Supporting line:
> Whether it’s a product, a film, or something that hasn’t been made before, I’m open to thoughtful collaborations.

Include the contact details supplied by the user or present in the existing project. Do not invent a phone number, email, or social profile. If no contact details are available, create clearly marked configuration fields.

The ending should feel like the final frame of a film: memorable, not cluttered.

## 4. Personal brand and Lumato Tech

Keep Isaac’s personal portfolio distinct from the Lumato Tech company website.

- **Isaac Lumato:** creative thinking, personal work, filmmaking, experiments, software projects, and professional reputation.
- **Lumato Tech:** commercial services such as software development, AI integration, business websites, AI-powered marketing, and technology education.

The portfolio may include a subtle Lumato Tech link or identify relevant work done through the company, but it must not become a corporate services brochure.

Do not force every project into a sales pitch. The purpose is to demonstrate Isaac’s range and point of view, then make collaboration easy.

## 5. Interaction and motion design

Motion should reinforce the narrative. It should not exist just to demonstrate that the developer knows an animation library.

Use a coherent motion language:
- Soft, deliberate entrance transitions.
- Subtle image reveals or masked transitions.
- Gentle parallax only where it improves composition.
- Carefully timed transitions between chapters.
- Clear hover and focus feedback.
- Thoughtful transitions between the selected work overview and a case study.
- Small editorial details, such as project indices or progress indicators, if useful.

Motion should feel deliberate, cinematic, and responsive—not bouncy, gimmicky, or excessively smooth for its own sake.

Important:
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Ensure the website remains complete and understandable with animations disabled.
- Avoid scroll hijacking.
- Avoid cursor effects that interfere with clicking or reading.
- Avoid long loading intros, mandatory sound, and autoplay audio.
- Do not make essential content dependent on hover.
- Ensure keyboard focus is visible.
- Avoid animations that cause layout shifts.

If the site uses video:
- Keep it muted by default.
- Provide a poster/fallback image.
- Avoid downloading multiple large videos immediately.
- Lazy-load noncritical media.
- Ensure mobile performance remains strong.

## 6. Technical expectations

First inspect the existing repository, framework, package manager, design system, assets, and routing. **Extend the existing project instead of replacing its stack unnecessarily.** Preserve working functionality.

If starting from scratch, choose a modern, maintainable React-based setup appropriate to the environment. Prefer the simplest architecture that delivers the intended quality.

Implementation priorities:
- Reusable components and clean separation of content from presentation.
- Consistent design tokens for color, spacing, type, motion, and layout.
- Responsive behavior from small mobile screens to large desktop displays.
- Semantic HTML and accessible interactions.
- Optimized images and responsive media.
- Lazy loading for below-the-fold media.
- Stable layouts with explicit image/video aspect ratios.
- SEO metadata, descriptive page titles, social sharing metadata, and a suitable favicon if assets allow.
- Sensible error and empty states.
- No dead links or fake buttons.
- No unnecessary backend or database for a mostly static portfolio.
- Easy-to-update project content, ideally through a simple data structure or content files.
- Clear component names and maintainable code.
- No secrets or API keys in frontend code.

Use Framer Motion or another existing motion solution if it fits the repository. Use Remotion only where code-driven video or animation genuinely benefits from it; do not add dependencies merely for prestige.

Do not add large dependencies for trivial effects. Avoid a heavy WebGL/3D scene unless it materially improves the design and performs well on real devices.

If an interactive detail cannot be implemented robustly, choose a simpler interaction and finish it properly.

## 7. Responsive and accessibility requirements

The site must feel intentionally designed on mobile—not like a compressed desktop site.

Check:
- Mobile navigation.
- Headline wrapping.
- Project media cropping.
- Comfortable tap targets.
- Text readability.
- Section spacing.
- Case-study navigation.
- Contact links.
- Reduced-motion behavior.
- Keyboard navigation and focus visibility.
- Color contrast.
- Screen-reader labels for meaningful controls.
- Alt text for meaningful images.
- Proper heading hierarchy.

Use responsive typography and fluid spacing where appropriate. Avoid horizontal overflow at common viewport widths.

## 8. Content integrity and evidence

This is a real personal portfolio, not a fictional design exercise.

Never fabricate:
- Client names or endorsements.
- Product screenshots or software features.
- Metrics, sales results, or performance gains.
- Awards or competition results.
- Project roles, ownership, or technical contributions.
- Live URLs, social accounts, or contact details.

Use existing verified content and assets. When something is missing:
1. Build a well-designed content slot.
2. Keep the missing content easy to add later.
3. Use neutral language rather than making unsupported claims.
4. Keep placeholders out of the published experience unless clearly intended as temporary development content.

Clearly distinguish personal experiments, client projects, collaborative work, and products. State Isaac’s role accurately for each project.

Do not publish confidential client information. Make it easy to omit or anonymize sensitive details.

## 9. Suggested information architecture

Use a small, purposeful site:

- `/` — The full portfolio narrative.
- `/work/[slug]` — Optional individual case studies, if the framework supports routing cleanly.
- `/about` — Optional dedicated About page if useful; otherwise keep About on the homepage.
- `/lab` — Optional archive for experiments if there is enough content; otherwise use a homepage section.

Do not create empty routes just to imitate a large website. A beautifully executed single-page portfolio with a few meaningful case-study pages is preferable to a sprawling, unfinished site.

The homepage should work as a complete story on its own.

## 10. What to avoid

Do not produce:
- A generic template with a gradient hero and three identical cards.
- A standard résumé disguised as a website.
- A wall of skill badges or a giant logo cloud of AI tools.
- A busy dashboard aesthetic.
- Excessive glassmorphism, neon colors, or gratuitous gradients.
- Random stock imagery used as fake project work.
- Endless scroll-triggered animations.
- Huge amounts of filler text.
- Fake statistics or inflated claims.
- Overly complex 3D effects that damage performance.
- A site that looks impressive in one screenshot but is awkward to use.
- A visually beautiful page with broken navigation, placeholder links, or incomplete mobile behavior.

The website should have a clear visual idea, not just many visual effects.

## 11. Definition of done

Do not consider the task finished until all applicable checks pass.

### Visual quality
- The site has a distinctive visual identity.
- It feels cinematic, editorial, artistic, and modern.
- The story unfolds in a deliberate sequence.
- Typography, imagery, spacing, and motion feel like parts of one design system.
- The selected projects receive appropriate visual emphasis.
- The final contact section provides a satisfying conclusion.

### Functionality
- All navigation and calls to action work.
- All project links point to real destinations or are clearly omitted until configured.
- No buttons are decorative but inert.
- No broken images or unexpected console errors.
- The page remains usable if media or motion fails.

### Engineering
- The existing stack is preserved where reasonable.
- Code is maintainable and content is easy to update.
- The site performs well on mobile.
- Accessibility and reduced-motion behavior are considered.
- Metadata and responsive image handling are implemented.
- No unsupported facts have been introduced.

### Verification
Run the available development server, build, lint, and type-check commands where available. Fix the issues they reveal. Inspect the rendered page at desktop and mobile sizes rather than trusting code alone. If browser or visual testing tools are available, use them.

---

## 12. How to work

1. Inspect the repository and identify the current framework, assets, and available commands.
2. Identify which real project media and content already exist.
3. Make a concise implementation plan internally, then start building.
4. Establish the design tokens, typography, and overall page composition first.
5. Build the story-driven homepage and responsive layouts.
6. Add project case-study presentation and the Lab section in a way that matches the available content.
7. Implement motion, accessibility, metadata, and performance refinements.
8. Run the available checks and inspect the result.
9. Fix incomplete or broken details before reporting completion.

Do not ask the user to decide every small design detail. Make thoughtful creative decisions. Ask a question only when an essential missing fact blocks a meaningful implementation; otherwise, use a clearly marked configurable field.

At the end, provide a concise summary of what was built, the main files changed, checks performed, and any genuine content/assets still needed from Isaac.

---

## Final creative test

Before finishing, ask yourself:

**Does this feel like a story about a person who builds things at the intersection of technology and imagination—or does it just look like another portfolio template?**

If it feels like a template, rethink the art direction and composition.

The goal is not to make the website shout.

The goal is to make it feel unmistakably considered.
